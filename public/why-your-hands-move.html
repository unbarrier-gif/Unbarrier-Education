Why your hands move

You said you doodle at work, and that you worry it looks like you have checked out. Here is what the research actually says about that.

The short version

Doodling and fidgeting are not what happens instead of concentrating. For a lot of people, they are part of how concentrating happens. The pen gives your brain a small, easy job so the rest of it can stay on the thing you are meant to be listening to.

The doodle is a bridge, not an escape. It carries you from one task to the next without your attention falling in the gap.

The study everyone quotes

Jackie Andrade, a psychologist at the University of Plymouth, ran an experiment in 2009. Forty people listened to a deliberately dull recorded phone message and had to note down who was coming to a party. Half of them were also given shapes to shade in while they listened. Afterwards, everyone got a surprise memory test.

29% more information recalled by the people who were shading shapes, compared with the people who just listened Andrade, Applied Cognitive Psychology, 2009

Andrade's explanation is that when a task is boring, the mind does not sit quietly and wait. It wanders off. Shading shapes takes up just enough attention to stop the wandering, but not enough to compete with the listening. Checking your phone is not the same thing, because that is a whole second task pulling in the other direction.

And the ADHD side of it

A separate line of research has looked at movement specifically. Sarver, Rapport and colleagues, working with boys aged eight to twelve, found that higher rates of movement were associated with better working memory scores in the children with ADHD — and slightly worse scores in the children without it. Their reading is that the movement is a way of keeping alertness up when the brain is being asked to hold and juggle information.

Julie Schweitzer's team at UC Davis has since reported similar patterns in adults. Their term for the small unconscious stuff — twirling, tapping, doodling, rocking — is intrinsic fidgeting, and they describe it as linked to better attention rather than worse.

Worth being straight with you: these are small studies, mostly done in labs, and mostly with children. They do not prove that doodling will fix a hard day at work. What they do show is that the assumption behind "stop fidgeting and pay attention" does not hold up, and that it may have things backwards for some brains.

What that means at work

You have been treating the doodling as something to hide. It is worth reconsidering that. Not as an excuse, and not as something to announce — just as a working method you can name if someone asks.

  1. Own the notepad Keep a plain pad next to you and let the pen move while you listen. If anyone asks, the honest answer is that it helps you hold on to what is being said.
  2. Use it as the gap between tasks Three to five minutes of doodling between two different jobs, rather than trying to switch cold. The switch is the hard part, not the work.
  3. Notice when the fidgeting steps up More movement than usual often means the task has got heavier or you have been at it too long. Treat it as information, not as a fault.
