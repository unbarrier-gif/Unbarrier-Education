Your prompt cycle

  1. Clean

    What is the learning goal?

    Write one sentence: “By the end, pupils will…” Separate the goal from the route.

    UDLKeep the goal fixed. The ways in can change.

  2. Consider

    What barrier could get in the way?

    Describe one barrier. Ask for different ways to engage, understand and show the same learning.

    UDLPlan the options before the barrier appears.

  3. Compare

    What is useful in each output?

    Give a second tool the same prompt. Read both through your barrier card.

    UDLWhich output offers more than one way in?

  4. Collect

    What is worth keeping?

    Choose the strongest parts. Check the science, language and accessibility before using them.

    UDLKeep the options, not just one version.

  5. Circle

    Did it reach the child?

    Try it with pupils. Look for more than one way to show the learning. Keep it, change one thing, or stop.

    UDLHow do we know? Look for more than one way out: said, shown, built or written.

The honest bit

A stronger prompt gives you a draft to judge. It takes time to check and try it. An AI output cannot tell you whether a pupil can use the resource.

UDL belongs inside consider

Universal Design for Learning means planning options before barriers appear.

  • Engagement

    What helps someone begin and stay involved?

    Try: support the first step.

  • Representation

    How else can the idea be understood?

    Try: a spoken script and a labelled visual.

  • Action and expression

    How else can someone show the learning?

    Try: spoken, written, or arranged and explained.

If pupils resist trying a different way, how do we help them try, build confidence and build stamina?

Barrier cards

Fictional situations, not diagnoses. Describe the barrier, never the child.

01Today

Card 1: Loses the thread after two lines

Long blocks of text. They stop reading and wait.

In the classroom

Break up text with icons and colour, so the meaning doesn’t rest on words alone.

Show a line to try

Use four numbered steps. Keep each step to two short sentences. Keep the stage names visible.

02Today

Card 2: New to English this year

They may know the idea in their first language. The English words are what gets in the way.

In the classroom

Use speech to text and immersive reader in the browser. Record your own voice so they hear how the words sound, then listen and repeat.

Show a line to try

Explain each key word in plain English and pair it with a labelled picture. Invite first-language notes, and check any translation.

03Today

Card 3: Finds it hard to start a task

A blank page or a big open task, and nothing happens.

In the classroom

Show what good looks like. Give three starting points, sentence starters and key vocabulary. Pair up: one shares, one listens and writes.

Show a line to try

Give one worked example and one clear first action. Offer the next step when the pupil is ready.

04Today

Card 4: Can’t hold a sequence in their head

They know the stages but lose the order.

In the classroom

Give them the stages to put in order and explain. Let them build the cycle.

Show a line to try

Keep a four-stage sequence visible. Use the same numbers and labels in every version, and show how the cycle repeats.

05

Card 5: Reading is well below age level

Words like evaporation and condensation block the meaning.

In the classroom

Use text tools to give different versions of the same information at different reading levels.

Show a line to try

Keep the scientific terms. Define each in a short sentence, add spoken support and cut unnecessary text.

06

Card 6: Spoken instructions don’t stay

They catch the first part of what’s said and lose the rest.

In the classroom

Record your voice once so they can play it back when they need it, in padlet, showbie or goodnotes. Record your own modelling too.

Show a line to try

Put each instruction in writing as well as saying it. Give one action at a time and leave it visible.

07

Card 7: Overwhelmed by a busy page

A crowded diagram with arrows, labels and colours, and they can’t find where to start.

In the classroom

Let them organise it digitally before committing to paper. Moving things around is easier, and nothing has to be perfect first time.

Show a line to try

Use one simple diagram with four main labels, a numbered starting point and plenty of space. Give a text version.

08

Card 8: Finds it hard to picture what they can’t see

Water vapour is invisible, so it’s hard to believe it’s there.

In the classroom

Create an image together, then check it. Vapour is invisible, so the picture needs labels and arrows.

Show a line to try

Explain that water vapour is an invisible gas. Use a familiar example, and show how it differs from the droplets in clouds or mist.

09

Card 9: Missed the last few lessons

The gap is the knowledge the lesson assumes they already have.

In the classroom

Share a short recording or one-page recap they can go through before the lesson.

Show a line to try

Start with a short recap of liquid water, water vapour, and heating and cooling. Check understanding before moving on.

10

Card 10: Anxious about getting it wrong

They won’t answer unless they’re sure. A quiz or being picked on shuts them down.

In the classroom

Reduce the stress, the load and the distractions, so the brain can do the work it needs to do. Let them think privately first.

Show a line to try

Offer private thinking time and a worked example. Let pupils rehearse with a partner or answer privately before sharing.

Prompts to copy · the water cycle

The ordinary prompt

Create a lesson explaining the water cycle for Year 4. Make it easier for the pupils who struggle.

Clean the goal

Plan a Year 4 lesson. By the end, pupils will be able to explain evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection in a repeating cycle. Keep this learning goal throughout.

The rebuilt prompt

Plan a Year 4 lesson. By the end, pupils will be able to explain evaporation, condensation, precipitation and collection in a repeating cycle.

Some pupils lose the thread after two lines. Some are new to English. Some find it hard to start or keep a sequence in mind. Keep the learning goal. Reduce these barriers.

Provide: (1) four numbered steps, with up to two short sentences each; (2) a simple labelled diagram with a text equivalent; (3) a short spoken explanation script. Define the four scientific terms in plain English. Keep the labels and order consistent. Give one worked example and a clear first action, with a visible overview.

Offer a choice of explaining aloud, in writing, or by arranging and explaining labelled stages. Use the same success criteria: name the four stages, explain what happens, and connect them in a repeating cycle.

Be scientifically accurate: water vapour is an invisible gas; clouds contain tiny water droplets or ice crystals. Explain the role of heating and cooling. Flag anything the teacher should check.

Go round again

Keep the learning goal and scientific vocabulary. The pupil still cannot begin. Replace the opening task with one action: point to evaporation on the diagram. Model one sentence about it, then invite the pupil to try. Keep the whole cycle available as an optional overview.

Use it in your own subject

Create a resource for [age or context]. By the end, learners will be able to [learning goal].

The barrier is [what gets in the way, without identifying anyone]. Keep the goal. Offer [ways to engage], [ways to understand] and [ways to show learning].

Use [format and length]. Include [first action or scaffold]. Check [subject facts and accessibility needs]. Flag assumptions for me to review.

The four-stage model is a useful simplification. The cycle has many routes and no fixed starting point. Check that any diagram does not show clouds as water vapour.

One prompt. One barrier. One week.

Pick one prompt you already use. Try the cycle once this week. Keep it, change it, or stop.
