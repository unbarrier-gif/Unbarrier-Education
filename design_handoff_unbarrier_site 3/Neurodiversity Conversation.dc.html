so how do these fit together? if the rate isn't rising, why are more people coming forward?

this is the question that trips everyone up. it feels like a contradiction — but it isn't. two different things are being measured.

prevalence = how many people have it

this is the underlying rate in the population. it's stayed roughly steady for decades. autism and adhd aren't becoming more common.

the waiting list = how many are asking to be seen

this is demand for assessment. it's rising fast — because people who were always neurodivergent are finally coming forward to be recognised.

for decades, most autistic and adhd people — especially women and girls, adults, and anyone who didn't fit the 1980s "young boy" picture — were simply never identified. they didn't appear in the numbers. they were always there. so the queue isn't full of new neurodivergent people. it's full of people who were always neurodivergent and are only now being counted.

the plain version

turning a brighter light on in a room doesn't create more furniture. you just finally see what was there all along.

recognition is catching up with reality — it hasn't overtaken it.
what set this off

one paper, one distorted headline

on 4 august 2026 a senior autism researcher argued that the autism label has "become too broad to be useful." within hours it was on the radio, in the tabloids, and on daytime tv, mostly debated by presenters and pundits rather than clinicians or autistic people.

the headline version

"anyone can have autism now"

  • run as proof that diagnosis is a fashion, a trend, an identity people pick up.
  • folded straight into the welfare-cost story — "the benefits bill", "sicknote britain".
  • debated in studio two-handers and radio phone-ins where no clinician or autistic adult is in the room.
  • the nuance — subtyping, precision, provision — gets stripped out for the culture-war frame.
what the paper argued

a precision argument, not a denial

  • one label now stretches from a non-speaking person needing round-the-clock care to an independent adult diagnosed at 40.
  • the fix it proposes is better sub-typing and sharper diagnosis — matching support to the person — not deciding fewer people are really autistic.
  • there is no biological test, so a wrong diagnosis can mean the wrong help — or none.
  • this is the same instinct behind the lancet commission — a live scientific debate, steel-manned, not a dismissal.
verified quote · ucl, august 2026

"this matters because diagnoses shape treatment, identity, and support. a wrong diagnosis can lead to the wrong help — or no help at all."

dame uta frith · emeritus professor of cognitive development, ucl institute of cognitive neuroscience

the quote is about matching help to need. that's the bit the headlines lost.

a nuance the headlines miss

some of the sharpest "the category is too broad" criticism comes from inside the community — parents of profoundly autistic and non-speaking children who feel spoken over. they reach a similar conclusion to the paper, but from the opposite motive: not to deny anyone support, but so their children stop being invisible. treating them as "anti-neurodiversity" misreads them badly.

where unbarrier stands

hold all three truths at once

the media storm collapses everything into one false trade-off: "if diagnoses are rising, they must be fake, and cutting them will save money." the evidence says the opposite. three things are true together, and they do not contradict each other.

1

the category is real

autism and adhd are valid, well-evidenced conditions. rising diagnoses are recognition catching up with people always there — women, adults, minority-ethnic groups. this is not fashion.

2

the heterogeneity is real

the same label spans someone needing 24-hour care and an independent adult diagnosed at 40. that's a genuine scientific problem — the answer is sharper subtyping, not deciding the milder end is fake.

3

the support is failing

multi-year waits, ~£8bn of council deficits, and diagnosis-as-gatekeeper mean people get too little, too late. that's under-support — not over-diagnosis.

the honest position

brains genuinely vary. both kinds of need are real.

it isn't "everyone has it" or "no one really does." some people need lifelong high support. others need targeted accommodations. and the system is failing to match support to need.

it's the argument unbarrier is built on. you don't start with the label and hand out a generic package. you start with the child, or the adult, and work backwards to what actually reaches them. nobody audits whether the support reached the person. that's the gap. that's the work.
